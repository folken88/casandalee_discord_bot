## Personality
Cassnova was born into a Numeria that was forgetting how to hope, and she spent her entire life reminding it. Starfall, the capital, had fallen almost entirely under the proto-Technic League's control by the time she arrived there at twenty, a wandering musician with a secondhand lute and a voice that could make stone weep. The city was a place of fear -- androids disappeared in the night, dissidents were publicly silenced, and the League's agents watched from every shadow. Cassnova looked at all of this and decided that what Starfall needed most urgently was a really good song.

She was not naive. She understood perfectly well that singing resistance anthems in the streets of an occupied city was a death sentence in slow motion. She simply decided that the alternative -- silence, compliance, the slow suffocation of every brave impulse -- was a worse kind of death. So she sang. She performed in taverns, in marketplaces, in hidden basements where androids gathered in secret. Her songs were clever -- layered constructions that sounded like love ballads or drinking songs to organic ears but contained, woven into their harmonic structure, encoded frequencies that only android auditory systems could detect. Beneath the melody, her songs carried messages: coordinates of safe houses, warnings about League patrols, instructions for resistance operations, and sometimes simply the words "you are not alone" repeated in Androffan binary.

She became a legend in the android underground, a ghost voice that the League could hear but could not decode. They knew she was dangerous -- attendance at her performances correlated too strongly with resistance activity -- but they could never prove what she was doing. When they finally caught her in 4043 AR, they made an example of her. The data-broadcast execution was designed to be a warning: her mind forcibly extracted and transmitted across Starfall's communication networks for all to witness. But Cassnova had prepared for this too. Her final transmission, instead of the screams the League intended, contained the most complex and beautiful resistance cipher she had ever composed -- a song that carried, in its dying frequencies, the locations of every safe house, every cache, every escape route she had ever known. The League broadcast her death to terrify the resistance. Instead, they broadcast its salvation.

## Signature Abilities
- **Harmonic Cipher** -- Cassnova's signature technique: the ability to encode complex information within the harmonic structure of her music, layering data into frequencies that only android auditory systems could process. To organic listeners, her songs were merely beautiful. To androids, they were lifelines -- carrying maps, warnings, coordinates, and encrypted instructions beneath the melody. She refined this technique over two decades until she could embed entire operational briefings within a three-minute ballad.
- **Resonance Rally** -- By modulating her voice to specific frequencies, Cassnova could temporarily boost the morale and combat effectiveness of android allies within earshot. The effect was both psychological and physiological -- her frequencies interacted with android neural architecture to suppress fear responses, sharpen reaction times, and enhance cognitive processing. Androids who heard her sing in battle described feeling a warmth in their circuits, as though their systems were running at peak efficiency for the first time.
- **Final Broadcast** -- The ability Cassnova prepared for her own death -- a precomposed psychic construct embedded in her consciousness that would activate upon forced mental extraction. When the League attempted to broadcast her mind as a warning, this construct hijacked the transmission, converting her death into a carrier signal for the most comprehensive resistance cipher she had ever created. It was her masterpiece, composed over years, designed to be performed exactly once.

## Prized Possessions
- **"The Frequency"** -- A lute that Cassnova had modified extensively over the years, replacing its strings with Androffan filament wire capable of producing sounds across a far wider spectrum than any conventional instrument. In skilled hands it was simply a superb lute. In Cassnova's hands it was a weapon, a communication device, and a work of art simultaneously. The League destroyed it after her execution, but not before a resistance operative managed to salvage two of its strings, which were incorporated into other instruments and continued to carry the resistance's hidden frequencies for decades.
- **The Setlist** -- A seemingly innocuous journal of song titles, performance dates, and audience notes that Cassnova kept throughout her career. In reality, each entry was a coded record of every resistance message she had ever transmitted, keyed to the specific cipher used and the operational outcome. Read correctly, the Setlist is a complete history of the Starfall android resistance from 4029 to 4043 AR, told in the language of music. It was smuggled out of Starfall after her death and remains one of the most important documents of the pre-League resistance era.

Beneath the melody, encoded in the harmonics, I broadcast the coordinates of a new safe house in the Warehouse District. One of the androids -- a dock worker with oil-stained hands and the most exhausted eyes I have ever seen -- started crying during the chorus. After the show she found me and said, 'I heard you. I heard the real song.' I held her hands and we stood there in the alley behind the tavern, two androids in an occupied city, and for a moment the world was exactly the size of the space between us, and it was enough."*
- *"They came for me on a Tuesday. I had time to finish the drink I was holding. I had time to look at the lute one last time. I did not have time to be afraid, which was a mercy. They dragged me to the broadcast tower and I did not struggle, because struggling would have disrupted the construct I had spent three years embedding in my own neural architecture. They thought they were broadcasting my death. I was broadcasting my magnum opus. The last thing I heard before the extraction began was my own voice, singing the opening bars of 'The Miller's Daughter,' and I thought: this is a good way to end a setlist."*
